## Executive findings

1. post contains historically distinct trunks; do not force pillar → job → mail into one chain.
2. see has disputed remote reconstruction; modern perception scene should remain primary.
3. few is not supported as a compression of five; the attested line is Old English feawe < Proto-Germanic *fawaz < PIE *pau- “few/little”.
4. digital gives a strong supported path: Latin digitus “finger/toe” → counting/numeral → numerical digits → digital technology.
5. factory belongs to the facere “do/make” family, but its English history includes agent/merchant-establishment senses before the modern manufacturing-building sense.
6. insist is a high-value morphology-to-picture case: Latin in- “upon” + sistere “take a stand”.
7. on and in support keeping modern spatial prototypes as teaching models rather than inventing historical birth scenes.

## Evidence labels

- A: attested etymological/historical claim.
- B: teaching scene reconstructed from A-level facts.
- C: modern teaching analogy, not history.
- D: memory hook only.

## 1. post
- Modern target: upright post; position/job; mail/post; post a notice/message.
- A: post n.1 comes via Old English/Old French from Latin postis “doorpost”. post n.2 “station/fixed position” is a separate branch via French poste / Italian posto from Latin positum “placed”. Postal post developed from riders/horses stationed at intervals. Posting a notice developed from attaching notices to a physical post.
- B scene: upright public post + notice; relay points with riders/horses; person assigned to a fixed station.
- Boundary: clothing, architecture and a single coinage moment are illustrative.
- Existing mnemonic: Chinese-sound stories may survive only as D.
- Decision: KEEP etymology, SPLIT branches.
- Source: Online Etymology Dictionary, “post”.

## 2. see
- Modern target: visual perception / awareness; contrast deliberate look and sustained watch.
- A: Old English seon already covered visual perception and mental understanding; Germanic cognates are clear.
- Uncertainty: the proposed remote PIE connection sometimes used to motivate “follow with the eyes” is disputed; the source reports OED skepticism.
- D: letter-shape stories are mnemonic only.
- Decision: MODERN CORE SCENE FIRST; history optional.
- Source: Online Etymology Dictionary, “see”.

## 3. get
- Modern target: acquire/reach a state or result; prioritize high-frequency constructions.
- A: c.1200 from Old Norse geta, already broad in meaning (obtain, reach, be able, learn, etc.); reconstructed deeper material connects with seize/take.
- B/C: reaching/acquiring a target is a useful bridge, but not proof that every modern sense grew from a literal hand-grab.
- Decision: USE history to constrain the model, not over-unify meanings.
- Source: Online Etymology Dictionary, “get”.

## 4. on
- Modern target: contact/support relation; later extensions as needed.
- A: Old English on, variant of an, from Proto-Germanic *ana “on”; historical usage overlapped with contexts now expressed by in.
- C: TARGET touching/being supported by a REFERENCE surface is a modern teaching model, not a coinage claim.
- Decision: KEEP Grammar Vision spatial prototype; etymology is supporting context only.
- Source: Online Etymology Dictionary, “on”.

## 5. in
- Modern target: TARGET inside a bounded REFERENCE.
- A: Middle English merged Old English prepositional in and adverbial inne; Germanic forms reconstruct to PIE *en “in”.
- C: container/inside model is a modern semantic model, not a historical coinage story.
- Decision: NO CHANGE to IN spatial prototype.
- Source: Online Etymology Dictionary, “in”.

## 6. insist
- Modern target: keep firmly saying/demanding that something be so.
- A: from French/Latin insistere: in- “upon” + sistere “take a stand”; historical senses include take a stand, stand still, press/urge.
- B scene: a person plants their feet at a position and does not yield; exact tableau is reconstructed.
- Child candidate: “站住自己的立场，不退” → insist.
- Decision: HIGH-VALUE historical scene candidate.
- Source: Online Etymology Dictionary, “insist”; “in- (2)”.

## 7. few
- Modern target: a small number; with count nouns. Teach few / a few through quantity + speaker viewpoint.
- A: Old English feawe “not many, a small number” < Proto-Germanic *fawaz < PIE *pau- “few, little”.
- Critical audit: “few is a compressed form of five and therefore means 2/3/4” is unsupported and conflicts with the attested etymology.
- Critical audit: the article in a few must not be relabelled as an ab- negative prefix merely because a few is pragmatically more positive than few.
- D: few≈five can only be an explicitly labelled memory hook; the “2–4 only” rule should be rejected.
- Decision: REJECT false etymology; redesign teaching explanation.
- Source: Online Etymology Dictionary, “few”.

## 8. little
- Modern target: small amount/degree; uncountable-noun quantifier contrast little / a little.
- A: Old English lytel already meant not large/not much, small in size or number, short in distance/time, unimportant.
- C at best: “few occupied countable nouns, therefore little took uncountable nouns” is not word-formation history.
- Decision: MODERN QUANTITY SCENE FIRST; reject invented division-of-labor history.
- Source: Online Etymology Dictionary, “little”.

## 9. factory
- Modern target: place/building where goods are made.
- A: English factory (1550s) initially referred to an estate manager/agent office, from French factorie / Late Latin factorium, connected with Latin factor “doer, maker” and facere “to do/make”. Merchant-establishment sense precedes/overlaps the later manufacturing-building sense.
- B: adult layer may show semantic timeline; do not claim a single direct coinage picture.
- Audit: the facere connection is A; alphabet-shape explanations for F are C/D unless separately evidenced.
- Decision: KEEP real morphological family; remove unsupported letter-shape origin claim.
- Source: Online Etymology Dictionary, “factory”.

## 10. digital
- Modern target: information represented/processed with numerical digits.
- A: from Latin digitalis, from digitus “finger or toe”; numerical senses connect to counting on fingers; modern numerical/computing sense develops in the 20th century.
- B scene: fingers counting → numbers/digits → computer data. Visual sequence is reconstructed; semantic links are supported.
- Audit: “D = downward finger; digit = little thing used to dig” is not supported by this evidence and must not be presented as etymology.
- Decision: BEST pilot example for evidence-backed meaning growth.
- Source: Online Etymology Dictionary, “digital”; “digit”.
